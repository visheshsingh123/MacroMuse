Smarter tracking

How to read food labels without getting lost in the numbers

Start with serving size, compare foods on the same basis, and use the label to make your meal log more useful.

By MacroMuse · 20 October 2026 · 3 min read

First, check what the numbers describe

Before looking at calories or protein, find the serving information. A label may show nutrition per serving, per 100 grams, per 100 milliliters, or more than one of these. The numbers make sense only when you know the amount they refer to.

A listed serving is a reference amount for the label. It is not a rule about how much you must eat. A packet may also contain several servings, even when it looks like something you could finish at once.

Match the label to the amount you ate

Use the same multiplier for calories and each nutrient. Suppose a snack label lists 150 kcal, 5 g protein, 20 g carbohydrate, and 6 g fat per 30 g serving. If you eat 60 g, that is two servings: 300 kcal, 10 g protein, 40 g carbohydrate, and 12 g fat.

If nutrition is listed per 100 g and you eat 40 g, multiply each number by 0.4. A kitchen scale can help with packaged foods, but a careful estimate is still useful when weighing is impractical. Record the portion you used so you can understand the entry later.

Compare foods using the same amount

Two products can look very different simply because their serving sizes differ. A cereal listed per 30 g serving cannot be compared directly with one listed per 45 g serving. Use a shared basis, such as per 100 g, or convert both to the portion you would eat.

Then consider the details relevant to the meal: protein, fiber, sodium, sugars, or the ingredients you prefer. There is no need to choose a food using one number alone. Taste, price, allergies, convenience, and the rest of your diet matter too.

Notice ingredients and preparation details

Check whether the values apply to the food as sold or after preparation. A drink powder mixed with milk has a different nutritional total from the powder alone. Instant noodles may list values for the dry product, a prepared serving, or the whole pack.

The ingredient list and allergen statement answer different questions from the nutrition table. Use them when choosing foods that fit your dietary needs. Label formats and reference percentages vary by country, so read the headings rather than assuming every package follows the same system.

Bring those details into your meal log

When logging packaged food, include the brand or product name, the portion you ate, and any additions. For example, “40 g cereal with 150 ml milk” is more useful than “a bowl of cereal.” If you ate only part of a pack, make that clear too.

A photo estimate may not identify a brand, recipe, or serving size correctly. Compare the estimate with the package information and correct your entry where needed. The label is usually a better starting point for that specific product than a generic estimate.

At your next meal, practice just the first two steps: find the basis and match it to your portion. Once those become familiar, the rest of the label is easier to use.
